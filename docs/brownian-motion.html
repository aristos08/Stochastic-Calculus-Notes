Work on a filtered probability space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\ge0},\mathbb{P})$.

Definition 2.1 (Standard Brownian motion).

An adapted process $B=(B_t)_{t\ge0}$ is a standard one-dimensional $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-Brownian motion if:

  1. $B_0=0$ almost surely;
  2. $B$ has continuous sample paths almost surely;
  3. for $0\le s\le t$, the increment $B_t-B_s$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_s$ and \[ B_t-B_s\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t-s). \]
Definition 2.2 (Gaussian process).

A process $(G_t)_{t\ge0}$ is called a Gaussian process if, for every finite collection of times $t_1,\ldots,t_n$, the random vector $(G_{t_1},\ldots,G_{t_n})$ has a multivariate normal distribution.

Proposition 2.3 (Gaussian characterization of Brownian motion).

Let $B$ be an adapted process with continuous paths almost surely. Relative to its natural filtration \[ \mathcal{F}_t^B:=\sigma(B_s:0\le s\le t), \] $B$ is Brownian motion if and only if:

  1. $B$ is a Gaussian process;
  2. $\mathbb{E}[B_t]=0$ for every $t\ge0$;
  3. $\mathbb{E}[B_sB_t]=s\wedge t$ for every $s,t\ge0$.
Proof.

Suppose first that $B$ is Brownian motion. Every finite vector $(B_{t_1},\ldots,B_{t_n})$ is Gaussian because it can be written as a linear transformation of the independent Gaussian increments. Also, $\mathbb{E}[B_t]=0$. If $s\le t$, then \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[B_sB_t] &=\mathbb{E}\bigl[B_s(B_s+B_t-B_s)\bigr]\\ &=\mathbb{E}[B_s^2]+\mathbb{E}[B_s(B_t-B_s)]\\ &=s, \end{align*} since $B_t-B_s$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_s$ and has mean zero.

Conversely, assume the three displayed properties. Since $\mathbb{E}[B_0^2]=0$, we have $B_0=0$ almost surely. For $s\le t$, the Gaussian variable $B_t-B_s$ has mean zero and variance \[ \operatorname{Var}(B_t-B_s)=t+s-2s=t-s. \] Hence $B_t-B_s\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t-s)$. Moreover, for every $r\le s$, \[ \operatorname{Cov}(B_t-B_s,B_r)=(r\wedge t)-(r\wedge s)=r-r=0. \] The vector $(B_t-B_s,B_{r_1},\ldots,B_{r_m})$ is jointly Gaussian whenever $r_1,\ldots,r_m\le s$. For jointly Gaussian random variables, zero covariance implies independence. Thus $B_t-B_s$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_s^B$, proving the Brownian-increment property.

∎

Filtration caveat. The preceding characterization proves independence from the natural filtration. If a larger filtration is used, one must additionally assume that it contains no information about future Brownian increments; otherwise the covariance conditions alone do not imply independence from $\mathcal{F}_s$.

Theorem 2.4 (Lévy's characterization).

Let $X$ be an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-adapted continuous process with $X_0=0$. Then $X$ is an $(\mathcal{F}_t)$-Brownian motion if and only if both $X$ and $(X_t^2-t)_{t\ge0}$ are martingales.

Proof of the “only if” direction.

If $X$ is Brownian motion, then it is a martingale because its increments are independent of the past and have mean zero. For $s\le t$, write $X_t=X_s+(X_t-X_s)$. Conditional on $\mathcal{F}_s$, the increment $X_t-X_s$ has mean zero and second moment $t-s$. Therefore \begin{align*} \mathbb{E}[X_t^2-t\mid\mathcal{F}_s] &=X_s^2+2X_s\mathbb{E}[X_t-X_s\mid\mathcal{F}_s] +\mathbb{E}[(X_t-X_s)^2\mid\mathcal{F}_s]-t\\ &=X_s^2+(t-s)-t=X_s^2-s. \end{align*} Thus $(X_t^2-t)_{t\ge0}$ is a martingale.

∎
Theorem 2.5 (Strong Markov property of Brownian motion).

Let $(B_t)_{t\ge0}$ be a Brownian motion with respect to a filtration $(\mathcal{F}_t)_{t\ge0}$ satisfying the usual conditions, and let $\tau$ be an almost surely finite stopping time. Then the shifted process \[ W_t:=B_{\tau+t}-B_\tau,\qquad t\ge0, \] is a Brownian motion independent of $\mathcal{F}_\tau$.

Equivalently, if $0\le t_1<\cdots<t_n$ and $h:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ is bounded and Borel measurable, then \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[ h\bigl(B_{\tau+t_1}-B_\tau,\ldots,B_{\tau+t_n}-B_\tau\bigr) \;\middle|\; \mathcal{F}_\tau \right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[h(B_{t_1},\ldots,B_{t_n})\right]. \]

Proof.

We first prove the result when $\tau$ is a simple stopping time, taking values $r_1,\ldots,r_m$. Let $A\in\mathcal{F}_\tau$. Since $A\cap\{\tau=r_j\}\in\mathcal{F}_{r_j}$, the independent-increments property of Brownian motion gives \[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \mathbf{1}_A h\bigl(B_{\tau+t_1}-B_\tau,\ldots,B_{\tau+t_n}-B_\tau\bigr) \right] \\ &\qquad = \sum_{j=1}^m \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \mathbf{1}_{A\cap\{\tau=r_j\}} h\bigl(B_{r_j+t_1}-B_{r_j},\ldots,B_{r_j+t_n}-B_{r_j}\bigr) \right] \\ &\qquad = \sum_{j=1}^m \mathbb{P}(A\cap\{\tau=r_j\}) \mathbb{E}\!\left[h(B_{t_1},\ldots,B_{t_n})\right] \\ &\qquad = \mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{E}\!\left[h(B_{t_1},\ldots,B_{t_n})\right]. \end{aligned} \] This is exactly the desired conditional-expectation identity.

For a general bounded stopping time $\tau$, approximate it from above by simple stopping times: \[ \tau_n:=2^{-n}\left\lceil 2^n\tau\right\rceil. \] Then $\tau_n\downarrow\tau$. Since Brownian paths are continuous, \[ B_{\tau_n+t_i}-B_{\tau_n} \longrightarrow B_{\tau+t_i}-B_\tau \qquad\text{almost surely} \] for each $i$. Applying the simple-stopping-time result to $\tau_n$ and passing to the limit using bounded convergence proves the result for bounded $\tau$. An arbitrary almost surely finite stopping time follows by applying the bounded case to $\tau\wedge N$ and then letting $N\to\infty$.

∎
Remark 2.6 (Interpretation).

The ordinary Markov property says that, after a fixed deterministic time $s$, the future process $(B_{s+t}-B_s)_{t\ge0}$ is a new Brownian motion independent of the past $\mathcal{F}_s$.

The strong Markov property says the same remains true if the time $s$ is replaced by a random time $\tau$, provided that $\tau$ is a stopping time: the decision that “now is time $\tau$” may use information from the past, but not information from the future.

Theorem 2.7 (Reflection principle).

Let $a>0$ and define \[ T_a:=\inf\{s\ge0:B_s=a\}, \qquad M_t:=\sup_{0\le s\le t}B_s. \] Then, for every $t>0$, \[ \mathbb{P}(M_t\ge a) = \mathbb{P}(T_a\le t) = 2\mathbb{P}(B_t\ge a). \] Consequently, \[ M_t\overset{d}{=}|B_t|. \]

Proof.

Since Brownian paths are continuous and $B_0=0<a$, \[ \{M_t\ge a\}=\{T_a\le t\}. \]

We now explain the reflection argument. Consider a path for which $T_a\le t$ and $B_t<a$. Such a path reaches the level $a$ for the first time at $T_a$, and then ends below $a$. Reflect the part of the path after this first hitting time across the horizontal line at height $a$: \[ \widetilde B_s:= \begin{cases} B_s, & 0\le s\le T_a,\\ 2a-B_s, & s>T_a. \end{cases} \] The reflected path has the same history up to $T_a$, but its terminal value is \[ \widetilde B_t=2a-B_t>a. \]

Why does this reflection preserve probabilities? By the strong Markov property, conditional on $\mathcal{F}_{T_a}$, the post-hitting process \[ W_u:=B_{T_a+u}-B_{T_a},\qquad u\ge0, \] is a fresh Brownian motion independent of $\mathcal{F}_{T_a}$. Brownian motion is symmetric, meaning \[ (W_u)_{u\ge0}\overset{d}{=}(-W_u)_{u\ge0}. \] But reflecting the original path after $T_a$ replaces $W$ by $-W$, because \[ \widetilde B_{T_a+u}-a = 2a-B_{T_a+u}-a = -(B_{T_a+u}-a) = -W_u. \] Hence reflection preserves the conditional distribution of the future given the past up to $T_a$.

Reflecting twice gives back the original path. Thus reflection pairs paths in \[ \{T_a\le t,\ B_t<a\} \] with paths in \[ \{B_t>a\}. \] The latter paths necessarily hit $a$ before time $t$, since they start at $0<a$ and end above $a$. Therefore, \[ \mathbb{P}(T_a\le t,\ B_t<a)=\mathbb{P}(B_t>a). \]

Now split according to the terminal value: \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(T_a\le t) &= \mathbb{P}(T_a\le t,\ B_t\ge a) + \mathbb{P}(T_a\le t,\ B_t<a) \\ &= \mathbb{P}(B_t\ge a)+\mathbb{P}(B_t>a). \end{aligned} \] Since $B_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,t)$ has a continuous distribution, \[ \mathbb{P}(B_t>a)=\mathbb{P}(B_t\ge a). \] Hence \[ \mathbb{P}(T_a\le t)=2\mathbb{P}(B_t\ge a). \]

Finally, for $x\ge0$, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(M_t\le x) &=1-\mathbb{P}(M_t>x) \\ &=1-2\mathbb{P}(B_t>x) \\ &=\mathbb{P}(|B_t|\le x). \end{aligned} \] Therefore $M_t\overset{d}{=}|B_t|$.

∎

For the proof, we check the algebra for the second definition of BM.

Corollary 2.9.

$\Prob( \inf\{ t > 0 \colon B_t = 0 \} = 0) = 1$. Ie, 0 is almost surely an accumulation point of zeroes of a BM. In fact, any zero is an accumulation point.

Theorem 2.10.

Almost surely, the path of a BM is of unbounded variation on any compact interval. (This is why Stochastic Calculus exists!!)

Proof.

$\{\Pi_n\}$ be an increasing sequence of partitions of $[a,b]$. Further, let $\{\Pi_n\}$ be such that the mesh of $\{\Pi_n\}$ goes to zero. Now, consider \begin{align*} &\left(\sum_{t_i \in \Pi_n} (B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i})^2\right) - (b-a) \\ &= \sum_{t_i \in \Pi_n} (B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i})^2 - (t_{i+1}-t_i) \\ \end{align*} Now, we will show that this goes to zero in $L^2$. All the terms within the sum are independent random variables with mean zero. This then shows that \begin{align*} &\E\left((\sum_{t_i \in \Pi_n} (B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i})^2 - (t_{i+1}-t_i))^2\right) \\ &= \sum_{t_i \in \Pi_n} \E((B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i})^2 - (t_{i+1}-t_i))^2 \end{align*} We know that \[ \frac{(B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i})^2}{t_{i+1}-t_i} \sim Z^2, \] where $Z \sim \mcal{N}(0,1)$. So, we then get \begin{align*} &\sum_{t_i \in \Pi_n} (t_{i+1}-t_i)^2 \E((Z^2-1)^2) \\ & \le \E((Z^2-1)^2) \sup_{t_i \in \Pi_n} |t_{i+1}-t_i| (b-a) \\ &\to 0, \end{align*} since the mesh of $\{\Pi_n\}$ goes to zero.

So, $\sum_{t_i \in \Pi_n} (B_{t_{i+1}} - B_{t_i})^2 \overset{L^2}{\to} b-a$.

Thus, there exists a subsequence $\{\Pi_n'\}$ such that we have almost sure convergence. On the other hand, \begin{align*} &\sum_{t_i \in \Pi_n'} (B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i})^2 \\ &\le \sup_{t_i \in \Pi_n'} |B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i}| \cdot \sum_{t_i \in \Pi_n'}|B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i}| \\ &\le \sup_{t_i \in \Pi_n'} |B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i}| \cdot \operatorname{TV}_{[a,b]}(B) \end{align*} Now, since $B$ is continuous on a compact interval, we can use uniform continuity, so that $\sup_{t_i \in \Pi_n'} |B_{t_{i+1}}-B_{t_i}| \to 0$. This means that the total variation cannot be finite, since if it was, the summation expression would converge to 0, which would not have $L^2$ norm going to $b-a$.

∎
